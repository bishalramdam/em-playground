Experiment 1 · Frequency modulation

How a song rides on a carrier

An FM station sends one steady radio wave, the carrier. To carry sound, it nudges the carrier's frequency up and down in step with the audio. Loudness sets how far it swings (the deviation). Pitch sets how fast it swings.

  • Turn on static in AM mode and watch the recovered audio get messy. Switch to FM: the radio clips the wave's height flat, so the static is thrown away.

  • Max out both: ±75 kHz and 15 kHz is the loudest, brightest thing a broadcast FM station may send.

Experiment 2 · Propagation

Bounce off the sky, or escape to space

80 to 400 km up, sunlight strips electrons off air molecules and makes the ionosphere. Low frequencies bounce off it and come back down far away. FM is too high a frequency to be reflected, so it goes straight through and keeps travelling into space at the speed of light.

Why the signal fades: the inverse-square law

A wave spreads over a growing sphere. At twice the distance the same energy covers four times the area, so each square metre gets a quarter of the power. At ten times the distance, a hundredth. Radio never "runs out"; it just gets thinner.

Good to know

  • FM and TV signals have been leaking into space since the 1940s. That "radio bubble" now reaches about 80 light-years out in every direction.
  • Deep-space missions use big dish antennas to focus power into a narrow beam, which is how NASA still hears Voyager 1's ~23 W transmitter from almost one light-day away.
Experiment 3 · Guided waves

A metal pipe for microwaves

A waveguide is a hollow metal tube. The wave bounces between its walls in a zig-zag and moves along with almost no loss. There is one catch: the tube must be at least half a wavelength wide. Below that cutoff frequency the wave cannot fit and dies out within a few millimetres.

Try this

  • Slowly lower the frequency toward cutoff. The zig-zag gets steeper, energy crawls forward, and crests race ahead faster than light. Crests carry no information, so physics is safe.
  • Press "FM in a pipe". At 98 MHz the tube would need to be 1.5 m wide. Instead the field fades out right at the entrance.
  • Pick TE₂₀ on the radar guide: it needs 13.1 GHz, so at 10 GHz only TE₁₀ travels. Designers choose sizes where exactly one mode fits.
Experiment 4 · Wave packets

Build a pulse from pure waves

A single sine wave goes on forever and carries no message. Add several waves with slightly different frequencies and they cancel almost everywhere, except in one bunch: a wave packet. Every radio pulse, Wi-Fi symbol and radar ping is a packet like this.

Try this

  • Start at 2 waves: you get endless beats. Add more and the repeats spread apart until one lonely pulse remains.
  • Widen the spread: the pulse gets shorter. Short pulses need wide bandwidth. That is why fast Wi-Fi needs wide channels.
  • Watch the orange dot (one crest) slide through the envelope. In a waveguide and in the ionosphere the ratio is below 1.
Experiment 4b · Wavelets

Fourier says which notes. Wavelets say when.

A Fourier spectrum lists the frequencies in a signal but loses their timing. A wavelet is a short wave packet used as a probe: slide it along the signal at many sizes and you get a map of which frequency happens when.

Try this

  • Flip between "Low, then high" and "Low + high together". The Fourier spectra look almost identical. The wavelet maps don't.
  • Pick "Tone with a click". The click is a thin vertical line on the map, but it's smeared across the whole Fourier spectrum.
  • Long wavelets give sharp frequency and blurry timing; short ones the opposite. You can't have both perfectly.
Experiment 5 · Wi-Fi

Why the bedroom has no signal

Wi-Fi is radio too, just 25 to 65 times higher in frequency than FM. Higher frequency means shorter waves, more loss with distance, and more loss through walls, but also room for much more data. Drag the router and the phone around this house.
