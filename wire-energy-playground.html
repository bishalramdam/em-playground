The energy travels around the wire, not inside it.

Electrons in copper drift slower than a snail. The energy that lights your room moves at nearly the speed of light, carried by electric and magnetic fields in the space around the conductors. Every playground here computes those fields from the charges and currents and shows where the energy actually goes.

Compared with a straight pair

The noise field from the motor cable pushes a small voltage around every loop the two wires make. In a twisted pair each twist flips the loop, so neighbouring loops push in opposite directions and cancel. The receiver only listens to the difference between the two wires, so what cancels here never reaches it.

Sending power across a country

A power line is a giant transmission line. At 50 Hz one wavelength is about 5,900 km, so a 1,000 km line holds a sixth of a wave, and strange things appear: voltage that rises at the far end with no load, power that can't get through, energy sloshing back and forth. Try this

  • Ferranti effect: 800 km at 400 kV, power at 0. The far end rises above the sending voltage, because the line's own capacitance keeps pushing charging current. Tick "shunt reactors" to tame it.
  • Surge impedance loading: on 300 km, set the power close to the SIL value at power factor 1.00. The voltage profile goes almost flat: the line's magnetic and electric energy balance, so it neither needs nor makes reactive power.
  • Voltage collapse: on a long line, keep raising the power. Past a point there is no solution: the line simply cannot deliver more. At 800 km even the SIL sits right at that edge, because nothing here props up the far-end voltage. Real grids add static var compensators and series capacitors; try the series compensation slider.
  • Go to 765 kV: four times the SIL of 400 kV. That is why long lines use very high voltages, and above roughly 600–800 km, HVDC is often cheaper.

When the air around a wire starts to glow

Right at the surface of a high-voltage conductor, the electric field is strongest. If it passes about 30 kV per centimetre, free electrons in the air gain enough speed between collisions to knock more electrons loose. An avalanche starts, the air ionises, and a faint violet sheath appears: corona. It hisses, hums at 100 Hz, makes ozone and radio noise, and burns real power.

The field picture of corona

In a healthy line, the Poynting vector runs parallel to the conductors: all the energy slides along toward the city. Near the surface, the same electric field that carries the energy is squeezed tight, because the charge sits on a small, curved surface (field ≈ charge ÷ radius). Where that field exceeds the air's breakdown strength, the air stops being an insulator. Ions now move, a current flows through the air, and E · J becomes positive there: the field does work on the ions. So the Poynting vector bends sideways into the glowing sheath, and that energy turns into light, heat, sound, ozone and radio noise.

Engineers fight it with the same field logic: fatter conductors or bundles of 2 to 4 spread the charge over a bigger, less curved surface, so the surface field drops below breakdown.

Try this

  • Set 400 kV with a single conductor: even in fair weather the air breaks down and the line hisses and loses tens of kW per km. Add rain and it glows. Switch to a bundle of 2 and it goes quiet. That is why every EHV line you see has bundled wires.
  • Press Rain. Water drops on the surface are tiny sharp points where the field spikes, so corona and losses jump.
  • Climb from sea level to Kathmandu, then to 4,000 m. Thinner air breaks down more easily, so mountain lines need bigger conductors.
  • Watch the close-up: the glow pulses twice per cycle, on the positive and negative peaks. That is where the 100 Hz hum comes from.
